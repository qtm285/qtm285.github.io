#| label: plot-error-distribution
#| echo: false
#| fig-alt: "Nine sampling distributions arranged by sample size (10, 40, and 160) and estimator (a, b, and c). Estimator a stays centered at one and narrows; estimator b approaches one from below and narrows; estimator c is a point mass at zero."
set.seed(1234)
error = 2*rnorm(100000)

target = 1
bias = list(a=function(n) 0, b=function(n) -10/(n+10), c=function(n) -1)
sds   = list(a=function(n) 1/sqrt(n), b=function(n) sqrt(n/(n+10)^2), c=function(n) 0)
plotdata = expand_grid(estimator=names(bias), n=c(10,40,160)) %>%
  pmap(function(estimator, n) {
    thebias = bias[[estimator]](n)
    thesd   = sds[[estimator]](n)
    data.frame(estimator=estimator, n=n, muhat=target + thebias + error * thesd)
    }) %>% bind_rows() 
summaries = plotdata %>% group_by(n, estimator) %>% summarize(mean=mean(muhat))

breaks = seq(-.5,2.5,by=.5)
ggplot(plotdata) + geom_histogram(aes(x=muhat, y=after_stat(density)), bins=100, alpha=.4) + 
                   geom_vline(aes(xintercept=mean), data=summaries) + 
                   scale_x_continuous(breaks=breaks, limits=range(breaks)) + 
                   facet_grid(n ~ estimator, labeller=labeller(n=label_both, estimator=label_both)) + 
                   theme_bw() + theme(axis.text.x = element_text(angle = -90, vjust = 0.5, hjust=1)) + 
                   xlab('') + ylab('') + ylim(0,3)

Problem 2

The miracle of random sampling is that we’re able to estimate the mean of a population with a very small sample from that population. But for that to work, our observations have to be independent—or close to it. If we observe the incomes \(Y_1 \ldots Y_n\) of \(n\) people drawn with replacement from a population with mean income \(\mu\) and income standard deviation \(\sigma\), the variance of the sample mean \(Y_1 \ldots Y_n\) will be \(\sigma^2/n\). Below, I’ve shown the calculation.

\[ \begin{aligned} \Var\sb*{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i} &= \E\sb*{ \cb*{ \frac{1}{n}\sum_i Y_i - \E \p*{ \frac{1}{n}\sum_i Y_i } }^2 } && \\ &= \E\sb*{ \cb*{ \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (Y_i - \E Y_i) }^2 } && \\ &= \E\sb*{ \cb*{ \frac{1}{n}\sum_i Z_i }^2 } && \text{for} \ \ Z_i = Y_i - \E Y_i \\ &= \E\sb*{ \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i \sum_j Z_i Z_j } && \\ &= \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_i \sum_j \E Z_i Z_j && \\ &= \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_i \sum_j \begin{cases} \sigma^2 & \text{ when } j=i \\ 0 & \text{ otherwise } \end{cases} \\ &= \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_i \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n^2} \times n \times \sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \end{aligned} \]

Now suppose that you’ve been lazy, and instead of calling \(n\) different people, you’ve just called one and reported their income \(n\) times. That is, you’ve got ‘a sample of size n’, \(\tilde Y_1 \ldots \tilde Y_n\), with \(\tilde Y_1=Y_1, \tilde Y_2 = Y_1, \tilde Y_3 = Y_1, \ldots\). What is the variance of the mean of this ‘sample’, \(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \tilde Y_i\)? And if it’s not \(\sigma^2/n\) like we got for the mean of \(Y_1 \ldots Y_n\), explain—with reference to the calculation above—why it is not. If there’s a line or lines where something different happens, say which; say what happens instead; and explain why.

do.thing = function(estimator) {
