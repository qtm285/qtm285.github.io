This chapter is optional and nothing in it is on an exam. It’s a breather.

We’ve calibrated interval estimates using exact sampling distributions. And using approximations to them: the bootstrap and the normal approximation. Do you really need a distributional approximation to do calibration? The answer is no. What you need are concentration inequalities: bounds on how far an estimate can land from the thing it estimates. For these, you don’t need its whole distribution, just a few facts about it, like its mean and variance.

The normal approximation happens pretty fast. But what do you do for real if you have a sample size of eight?

An Expectation Alone Bounds a Probability: Markov’s Inequality

Recall: Indicators

The indicator is 1 for the points inside the blue region and 0 for the points outside. The mean of the indicators is the fraction of points inside. That is the tool from Means, Variances, and Sample Averages, used on an event of the form X\ge t: \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{\ge t}(X)]=P(X\ge t).

How Much Can Lie Above t?

Suppose X\ge0 and we know \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X]. For t>0, how large can P(X\ge t) be?

Each dot’s area is now the value of the indicator at that dot: zero below t, one at or above it, and the blue step drawn over the histogram is what that draws. The indicator counts the tail. Can we bound it using a multiple of x?

For x\ge0, the ramp u_t(x)=x/t stays above the indicator: 1_{\ge t}(x)\le u_t(x).

The horizontal axis still shows x. Height now shows the value represented by shade and dot area. For every nonnegative x, \begin{aligned} x\ge t &: \quad 1_{\ge t}(x)=1\le x/t,\\ 0\le x<t &: \quad 1_{\ge t}(x)=0\le x/t. \end{aligned}

Averages Keep the Order

If a(x)\le b(x) for every possible value x of X, then \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[a(X)]\le \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[b(X)]. The proof is just the definition of expectation. Every difference b(x)-a(x) is nonnegative, and every probability is nonnegative, so \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[b(X)]-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[a(X)] =\sum_x \{b(x)-a(x)\}P(X=x)\ge 0.

Markov’s Inequality

For every nonnegative random variable X and t>0, \begin{aligned} P(X\ge t) &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{probability as expectation}}{=}\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{\ge t}(X)]\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{the ramp bounds the indicator}}{\le}\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[u_t(X)]\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{the bounding ramp}}{=}\mathop{\mathrm{E}}\sb*{\frac Xt}\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{linearity}}{=}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X]}t. \end{aligned}

Versatility

Back to a question from Means, Variances, and Sample Averages: does the expectation tell us where the distribution is located, i.e. where draws are likely to be? If not, why is that useful? Here are three shapes with the same picture drawn on each — a single bump, then flips, then two bumps — with the ramp shade across the panel, the green line at the mean, and the cutoff at t dotted.

On the flips and on the two bumps the mean is nowhere a dot lands. This is not location in the traditional sense, and Markov’s inequality still works: the bound needs the mean and nonnegativity, and neither of those asks where the draws are.

For a Sample Mean

Can we use Markov to calibrate an interval for a population mean? Consider a binary response with population frequency \theta and sample frequency \hat\theta, using uniform sampling as above. We know \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[\hat\theta]=\theta, and \hat\theta is nonnegative, so Markov applies to it. Taking the threshold t=20\theta, P(\hat\theta\ge20\theta)\le\frac{\theta}{20\theta}=0.05. So with probability at least 95\%, our sample frequency is below 20\theta. That is a statement about \hat\theta, which we observe; it rearranges into one about \theta, which we do not. \theta\ge0.05\hat\theta \qqtext{ with probability at least } 95\% If our observed frequency is 0.7, we learn that \theta\ge0.035. A frequency cannot exceed one, so our guaranteed 95\% interval is [0.035,1]. We have ruled out almost nothing.

That’s a one-sided interval, like the ones in the power deck. Markov bounds the probability of landing far above the mean, so what we get is a lower bound on \theta.

Compare it with a one-sided interval calibrated using the binomial. For binary responses, resampling from the observed sample produces the binomial model with its success probability set to \hat\theta. That one narrows as the sample grows. The Markov interval above does not: the expectation of the sample frequency remains \theta for every sample size.

The panels show the same construction for three sample sizes. Each purple dot is a sample frequency. For one highlighted sample, the one-sided bootstrap/binomial interval is pink; the dark interval is Markov’s. The green line is the population frequency. Both intervals use the same sample.

The Markov guarantee is valid without knowing the full sampling distribution, and even without independence. The bootstrap/binomial calibration uses more information and is much more informative here; its plug-in calibration is not itself a distribution-free guarantee. Our one-sided interval says that \theta is not much smaller than what we observed, and nothing at all about its being much larger. To get both sides out of Markov we would have to apply it to a quantity that is large whenever our error is large in either direction. Squared error is such a quantity, and that’s where we go next.

Chebyshev’s inequality

We do not need a new inequality. Markov’s applies to any nonnegative random variable, and above we fed it our estimate. That gave one side only, a bound on landing far above the mean and nothing about landing far below:

The distance from the mean is different. Write Z=X-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X] for the centered outcome. The distance is large when our estimate lands far above the mean and also when it lands far below, so bounding its size bounds both sides at once. And Markov will take it, because a distance is never negative.

There are two obvious ways to turn our error into a distance. We can take the absolute value, |Z|, or we can square it. Absolute values are unpleasant to calculate with — they do not come apart when we add things up, and adding things up is most of what we do here. Squaring is just arithmetic by comparison. So let’s square.

From distance to squared distance

The events |Z|\ge t and Z^2\ge t^2 select exactly the same observations. We want to bound the probability that X is far from its mean. In the first picture, select the observations at distance at least t on either side. Fold the negative side onto the positive side; those same observations are now above t. Square the distances and the threshold.

Signed distances. Select observations at distance at least t from zero, on either side. These are precisely the observations at distance at least t from the original mean.

Fold. Replace each distance by its absolute value. The selected observations are now at or above t; their number and probability have not changed.

Square. Square every absolute distance and square the threshold. The same observations are now at or above t^2, and every value is nonnegative.

Chebyshev’s inequality is Markov’s inequality applied to squared distance: for t>0, P\left(|X-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X]|\ge t\right) \le\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(X-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X])^2]}{t^2}.

The first two pictures changed the event’s description without changing which observations it contains. The indicator-to-ramp comparison bounds its probability:

Indicator average. Assign one to each selected observation and zero to every other observation. The average is the probability we want to bound: P(Z^2\ge t^2)=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{\ge t^2}(Z^2)].

Ramp average. Replace the indicator by squared distance divided by t^2. Every value is at least as large, so its average bounds the indicator average: \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{\ge t^2}(Z^2)]\le\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z^2/t^2].

Putting those together, \begin{aligned} P\left(|X-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X]|\ge t\right) &=P(Z^2\ge t^2)\\ &=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{\ge t^2}(Z^2)]\\ &\le\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z^2/t^2]\\ &=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z^2]}{t^2}. \end{aligned}

Calibrating intervals with variance

Measure distance in multiples of \sigma.

Standard deviation is standard

Write \sigma=\operatorname{sd}(X)>0. Chebyshev implies P\left(|X-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X]|\ge k\sigma\right)\le\frac1{k^2}. The bound has the same form for every distribution with finite variance. If \sigma=0, X equals its expectation with probability one and there is no deviation to bound.

Measuring the deviation in standard deviations is what makes the bound usable: the width scales with \sigma, and the probability outside it does not depend on the distribution at all.

So how many standard deviations include at least 95\% of a distribution’s probability? Solve 1/k^2=0.05 to get k=\sqrt{20}. For an unbiased estimator \hat\mu, that makes [\hat\mu-\sqrt{20}\operatorname{sd}(\hat\mu),\;\hat\mu+\sqrt{20}\operatorname{sd}(\hat\mu)] an interval covering \mu with probability at least 95\%. For an independent sample mean, \operatorname{sd}(\hat\mu)=\sigma/\sqrt n, so the width shrinks like 1/\sqrt n — which is what Markov’s bound never did.

A sample of eight

Back to the question we started with. Make eight calls, with replacement, to a population where half say Yes, so \theta=1/2 and \sigma^2=1/4. Here are three ways to get a 95\% interval for \theta, and how often each one actually covers it.

half-width actual coverage
normal approximation, 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt n 0.346 93.0\%
exact, from the binomial 0.375 99.2\%
Chebyshev, \sqrt{20}\,\sigma/\sqrt n 0.791 100\%

The normal approximation is narrow and wrong: it claims 95\% and delivers 93\%, and nothing in the procedure tells you so. Chebyshev is right and useless: its interval is wider than the range \hat\theta can take. The exact interval covers 99.2\% rather than 95\% because with eight calls coverage moves in steps; you can’t buy exactly 95\% from a discrete distribution.

Extra credit: Markov on the fourth moment

Chebyshev is Markov applied to the squared error. Apply it to the fourth power instead: P(|\hat\theta-\theta|\ge t)\le\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(\hat\theta-\theta)^4]}{t^4}. To calculate \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(\hat\theta-\theta)^4], expand the fourth power of \frac1n\sum_i Z_i the way we expanded the square. With independent calls, a term \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_iZ_jZ_kZ_l] is zero unless its indices pair up: all four the same, which happens n ways, or two different pairs, which happens 3n(n-1) ways. So \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(\hat\theta-\theta)^4]=\frac{n\,\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z^4]+3n(n-1)\sigma^4}{n^4}, and for binary responses \sigma^2=\theta(1-\theta) and \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z^4]=\theta(1-\theta)(1-3\theta+3\theta^2).

With eight calls and \theta=1/2 that is 11/4096, so the 95\% half-width is (11/4096 \div 0.05)^{1/4}=0.481. It covers 99.2\%, like the exact interval, and it is about a quarter wider. Unlike the exact interval, it needs only two moments, and it is a guarantee at every sample size. The bound shrinks like 1/n^2, where Chebyshev’s shrinks like 1/n.

This calculation used independence to kill the terms that don’t pair up, so it is for sampling with replacement.

How narrow can the right interval be?

Chebyshev tells us a width that is sufficient. Can the moments also tell us how narrow a calibrated interval could be? Almost: we need two summaries of the absolute error, its mean and its mean square.

Return to the two distance pictures. They follow the same centered observations Z. The green lines mark two different averages: the average of |Z| and the average of Z^2.

Average absolute distance. Every observation’s distance from the mean, averaged; the green line marks \mathop{\mathrm{E}}|Z|. A point twice as far contributes twice as much.

Average squared distance. The same distances, squared before averaging; the green line marks \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z^2], the variance. Squaring makes far points count quadratically more — twice as far is four times the contribution — which is what makes this average sensitive to the largest errors.

For a nonnegative random variable A with finite, positive second moment, the Paley–Zygmund inequality implies, for 0\le t<\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[A], P(A\ge t)\ge\frac{(\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[A]-t)^2}{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[A^2]}. Apply it to A=|\hat\mu-\mu|. For an unbiased estimator, \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[A^2] is its variance; \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[A] is its mean absolute error. This supplies lower control of the tail using those two moments. A derivation using the indicator split and Cauchy–Schwarz is on Wikipedia.

For 95\% coverage, any symmetric half-width smaller than \max\left\{0,\;\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[A]-\sqrt{0.05\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[A^2]}\right\} misses more than 5\% of the probability. The dashed yellow intervals below mark that lower control of width; they are not another proposed 95\% interval. The exact calibrated widths lie between it and the sufficient Chebyshev widths.

Why is variance alone insufficient? Consider an error that is zero with probability 95\% and is -L or L with probability 2.5\% each. Its mean is zero and its variance is 0.05L^2. Push the two tails farther out: the variance grows, but the interval containing just zero still contains 95\% of the probability. A large variance need not mean that most errors are large.

Tail distance 1. The error is 0 with probability 95% and \pm 1 with probability 2.5% each. Mean 0; variance 0.05.

Tail distance 2. The tails move out; the probabilities do not. A narrow interval around zero still contains 95% of the probability, and the variance has quadrupled to 0.2.

Tail distance 4. Tails at \pm 4; variance 0.8 — sixteen times the first picture’s — and zero-alone still covers 95%.

Tail distance 8. Tails at \pm 8; variance 3.2, sixty-four times the first. A large variance has not made the typical error large — which is the point: the variance alone cannot narrow a calibrated interval.

The lower-bound comparison therefore uses the mean absolute error as well as the variance. The binomial and hypergeometric examples let us calculate both, and place the exact calibrated width between the lower control and the sufficient Chebyshev width.
