Summary

The question is whether a turnout survey can help us predict who wins. Taking each group’s candidate preference as given lets us isolate the uncertainty about who shows up. That makes the ratio an election forecast, the confidence interval a statement about whether we can call a majority, and the power calculation a way to plan a survey capable of doing that.

In class, we discussed the Georgia Republican Party’s effort to suppress Black turnout. Could that effort cost the Democrats their majority? For this exercise, we’ll take Democratic support to be 90% among Black voters and 35% among non-Black voters. These are hypothetical inputs. Suppose that effort reduces Black turnout from 60% to 58%, with the same registration counts and non-Black turnout. This lowers the implied Democratic vote share from about 50.05% to 49.68%. The candidate moves from just above one-half to just below it. We want to know whether a turnout survey can give convincing evidence of that loss of a majority.

In our lecture Comparing Two Groups: Precision, Power and Allocation, we used the variance of a difference in subsample means to work out how many calls our survey needs. A candidate’s vote share depends on turnout in each group, because a group that turns out more makes up more of the people who vote. Here you’ll use a linear approximation to work out the variance of that forecast and put a confidence interval around it. We’ll review the lecture’s power calculation, then you’ll adapt it to the vote share and work out how many calls we need, and how to divide them between the groups, to have a good chance of showing that the Democratic vote share is below one-half under the reduced-turnout alternative.

Calculus Review: Linear Approximation

We’re going to be using linear approximation to simplify some of our calculations. Given a function h(x), we can approximate it near any point x_0 like this. We call x_0 the base point: it is where we evaluate the function and its derivative. h(x) \approx h(x_0) + h'(x_0)(x-x_0)

Hopefully you remember that from calculus. If you like, you can call this first-order Taylor approximation. And there are a few formulas for the error of this approximation, which is called the remainder in Taylor’s Theorem, in most calculus textbooks.

When we’re thinking about functions of multiple variables, we use the multivariate version, which involves partial derivatives. Here the base point is (x_0,y_0), where we evaluate the function and its partial derivatives. \begin{aligned} h(x,y) &\approx h(x_0,y_0) \\ &\quad + \left\{\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}(x_0,y_0)\right\} (x-x_0) \\ &\quad + \left\{\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}(x_0,y_0)\right\} (y-y_0). \end{aligned}

Variance Calculation for Comparisons

Differences in Means

In our lecture Comparing Two Groups: Precision, Power and Allocation, we talked about how to use subsample means to compare two groups. In particular, we talked about the case that we’ve drawn a stratified sample of Georgia’s registered voters: we chose two sample sizes in advance, n_1 Black registered voters (w=1) and n_0 non-Black registered voters (w=0), and drew a sample independently from each group, without replacement. And we talked about using the difference \textcolor{#00BFC4}{\hat\mu(1)}-\textcolor{#F8766D}{\hat\mu(0)} in the turnout rates among the n_1 and n_0 sampled voters to estimate the corresponding difference \textcolor{#00BFC4}{\mu(1)}-\textcolor{#F8766D}{\mu(0)} in turnout among the m_1 and m_0 registered voters. The rest of this homework uses two facts about this estimator from that lecture. First, it’s unbiased: expectation is linear, and each subsample mean is an unbiased estimator of its group’s turnout rate when we sample from that group with or without replacement (Homework 4).

\operatorname{E}\left\{\textcolor{#00BFC4}{\hat\mu(1)}-\textcolor{#F8766D}{\hat\mu(0)}\right\} =\operatorname{E}\left\{\textcolor{#00BFC4}{\hat\mu(1)}\right\}-\operatorname{E}\left\{\textcolor{#F8766D}{\hat\mu(0)}\right\} =\textcolor{#00BFC4}{\mu(1)}-\textcolor{#F8766D}{\mu(0)}.

Second, because we draw the two samples independently, their variances add (Homework 4, Variances), and each one has the correction factor for sampling without replacement that you derived in Homework 4.

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{V}\left\{\textcolor{#00BFC4}{\hat\mu(1)}-\textcolor{#F8766D}{\hat\mu(0)}\right\} &=\operatorname{V}\left\{\textcolor{#00BFC4}{\hat\mu(1)}\right\}+\operatorname{V}\left\{\textcolor{#F8766D}{\hat\mu(0)}\right\}\\ &=\textcolor{#00BFC4}{\frac{\sigma^2(1)}{n_1}}\textcolor{#ef476f}{\frac{m_1-n_1}{m_1-1}} +\textcolor{#F8766D}{\frac{\sigma^2(0)}{n_0}}\textcolor{#ef476f}{\frac{m_0-n_0}{m_0-1}}. \end{aligned}

Because turnout is binary, \sigma^2(w)=\mu(w)\{1-\mu(w)\}. Statewide the correction factors are close to one and we can get away with dropping them. That’s what we did in class. And you can confirm that dropping them was OK by plugging in m_1, m_0, n_1 and n_0. In McIntosh County, later in this homework, they aren’t.

Ratios of Means

Write \theta_1 and \theta_0 for the Democratic candidate’s share of the votes cast by Black and by non-Black registered voters. We’ll take \theta_1 = 0.90 and \theta_0 = 0.35 as given, so the only thing we’re estimating is turnout. These are round figures chosen for this exercise. Of the m_1 = 2{,}290{,}365 Black registered voters on Georgia’s 2020 roll, m_1\mu(1) vote, and m_1\mu(1)\theta_1 of them vote for the candidate; likewise for the m_0 = 5{,}350{,}986 non-Black registered voters on that roll. So the candidate’s share of the votes cast is \theta=\frac{m_1\mu(1)\theta_1+m_0\mu(0)\theta_0}{m_1\mu(1)+m_0\mu(0)}.

NoteWhy Black and non-Black?

“Non-Black” is everyone else on the roll: a complement, defined by what its members are not. So it is worth asking why splitting the state two ways is a reasonable thing to do at all.

The figure follows a few of the larger groups the Times reports on, at three elections. What matters here is how far apart they are. Black voters sit around D+80, far from everyone else shown; the other groups all sit much nearer even.

That distance is what makes the two-way split worth anything. D+80 is roughly a 90–10 split, which is where our \theta_1 = 0.90 comes from. We choose \theta_0 = 0.35 as a hypothetical support level for the exercise. With our turnout assumptions, it lets the two-point drop in Black turnout move the statewide Democratic vote share from just above one-half to just below it. The 35% is not estimated from the Times figure, which is national rather than Georgian. We take both group support rates as given so that we can study the uncertainty about turnout.

If \hat\mu(1) and \hat\mu(0) are good estimators of \mu(1) and \mu(0), then shouldn’t this be a good estimator of \theta? \hat\theta = \frac{m_1\hat\mu(1)\theta_1+m_0\hat\mu(0)\theta_0}{m_1\hat\mu(1)+m_0\hat\mu(0)} Let’s look into it. To do this, we’ll think of the vote share as a function of the two turnout rates. \hat\theta-\theta = h(\hat\mu(1),\hat\mu(0))-h(\mu(1),\mu(0)) \quad\text{for}\quad h(x,y)=\frac{m_1\theta_1x+m_0\theta_0y}{m_1x+m_0y}.

And we’ll use a linear approximation to this function to think about this difference.

We choose the base point for convenience. For theoretical calculations of bias and variance, we’ll use the population turnout rates (\mu(1),\mu(0)), so the coefficients stay constant as the sample changes. When we estimate the variance from a sample, we’ll plug in the sample turnout rates, because those are the ones we have. Either way, there is an approximation error to bound.

Using the population turnout rates as our base point, we get this.

\begin{aligned} h(\hat\mu(1), \hat\mu(0)) &\approx h(\mu(1), \mu(0)) \\ &\quad + \left[\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial x}(\mu(1), \mu(0))\right]\left\{\hat\mu(1) - \mu(1)\right\} \\ &\quad + \left[\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial y}(\mu(1), \mu(0))\right]\left\{\hat\mu(0) - \mu(0)\right\} \end{aligned}

This approximation should be good if \hat\mu(1) and \hat\mu(0) are close to \mu(1) and \mu(0).

Exercise 23.1  

Exercise

When our sample includes a reasonably large number of observations in both groups, it’s reasonable to expect that they should be close. With a sentence or two, or a rough sketch if you prefer, explain why.

SolutionSolution

If we have a large sample size in group w, then \hat\mu(w)-\mu(w) will be small with very high probability. It’s approximately normal with mean zero and standard error

\mathrm{se}_w=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2(w)}{n_w}\frac{m_w-n_w}{m_w-1}}.

This means that the difference should be smaller than 2\,\mathrm{se}_w in 95% of samples and smaller than 3\,\mathrm{se}_w in more than 99% of samples.

Now that we’ve justified the approximation, let’s use it to analyze our vote share estimator.

SolutionSolution

We’ll start by calculating the partial derivatives that appear in our linear approximation. For

h(x,y)=\frac{m_1\theta_1x+m_0\theta_0y}{m_1x+m_0y},

the quotient rule gives \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial h}{\partial x} &=\frac{m_1\theta_1(m_1x+m_0y)-(m_1\theta_1x+m_0\theta_0y)\,m_1}{(m_1x+m_0y)^2} \\ &=\frac{m_1m_0(\theta_1-\theta_0)\,y}{(m_1x+m_0y)^2}, \\ \frac{\partial h}{\partial y} &=\frac{m_0\theta_0(m_1x+m_0y)-(m_1\theta_1x+m_0\theta_0y)\,m_0}{(m_1x+m_0y)^2} \\ &=-\frac{m_1m_0(\theta_1-\theta_0)\,x}{(m_1x+m_0y)^2}. \end{aligned} In each numerator, the terms in m_1^2 or m_0^2 cancel. Evaluating the partial derivatives at the population base point (\mu(1),\mu(0)) and plugging them into our linear approximation, we get this. \begin{aligned} \hat\theta&\approx\theta+c_1\left\{\hat\mu(1)-\mu(1)\right\}+c_0\left\{\hat\mu(0)-\mu(0)\right\} \\ \quad\text{for}\quad c_1&=\frac{m_1m_0(\theta_1-\theta_0)\,\mu(0)}{\left\{m_1\mu(1)+m_0\mu(0)\right\}^2} \\ \quad\text{and}\quad c_0&=-\frac{m_1m_0(\theta_1-\theta_0)\,\mu(1)}{\left\{m_1\mu(1)+m_0\mu(0)\right\}^2}. \end{aligned} To approximate our bias, we take the expectation of both sides. \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\left(\hat\theta\right) &\approx \theta + c_1 \operatorname{E}\left\{\hat\mu(1) - \mu(1)\right\} + c_0 \operatorname{E}\left\{\hat\mu(0) - \mu(0)\right\} \\ &=\theta + c_1 \times 0 + c_0 \times 0 = \theta \end{aligned} Subtracting \theta from both sides, it follows that our bias, \operatorname{E}\left(\hat\theta\right) - \theta, is approximately zero.

Evaluating the partial derivatives there gives \hat c_1 \approx 0.1848 and \hat c_0 \approx -0.1582. Separately, we estimate each group’s population turnout variance by its empirical variance, \hat\sigma^2(w)=\hat\mu(w)\left\{1-\hat\mu(w)\right\}. \begin{aligned} \widehat{\operatorname{V}}\left(\hat\theta\right) &=\hat c_1^2 \frac{\hat\sigma^2(1)}{n_1} + \hat c_0^2 \frac{\hat\sigma^2(0)}{n_0} \\ &= (0.1848)^2 \frac{0.5840 \times 0.4160}{5{,}259} + (-0.1582)^2 \frac{0.6822 \times 0.3178}{12{,}270} \\ &\approx 0.00000202 \\ &\approx 0.00142^2 \end{aligned}

SolutionSolution

Write \Delta_1=\hat\mu(1)-\mu(1) and \Delta_0=\hat\mu(0)-\mu(0) for the turnout errors, and V_w=\operatorname{V}\{\hat\mu(w)\}=\frac{\sigma^2(w)}{n_w}\frac{m_w-n_w}{m_w-1} for their variances. Write N=m_1\mu(1)\theta_1+m_0\mu(0)\theta_0 for the number of votes for the candidate and D=m_1\mu(1)+m_0\mu(0) for the number of votes cast, so \theta=N/D, and write \hat N and \hat D for the same expressions with \hat\mu(1) and \hat\mu(0).

Expanding h to second order

Following the hint, we carry the two-variable expansion of h one term further. Taylor’s theorem with the Lagrange form of the remainder says that for some point (\tilde x,\tilde y) on the segment between (\mu(1),\mu(0)) and (\hat\mu(1),\hat\mu(0)),

\hat\theta =\theta+c_1\Delta_1+c_0\Delta_0 +\frac12\left\{\frac{\partial^2h}{\partial x^2}\Delta_1^2 +2\frac{\partial^2h}{\partial x\,\partial y}\Delta_1\Delta_0 +\frac{\partial^2h}{\partial y^2}\Delta_0^2\right\},

with the second derivatives evaluated at (\tilde x,\tilde y). Call the last term R, the remainder. The linear terms have expectation zero, so the bias is exactly \operatorname{E}(R), and |\operatorname{E}(\hat\theta)-\theta|\le\operatorname{E}|R|.

To find the second derivatives, write D(x,y)=m_1x+m_0y for the denominator of h, so D=D(\mu(1),\mu(0)) and \hat D=D(\hat\mu(1),\hat\mu(0)). Differentiating the partial derivatives from Exercise 23.2 once more with the quotient rule gives

\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2h}{\partial x^2}&=-\frac{2m_1^2m_0(\theta_1-\theta_0)\,y}{D(x,y)^3}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2h}{\partial y^2}=\frac{2m_1m_0^2(\theta_1-\theta_0)\,x}{D(x,y)^3},\\ \frac{\partial^2h}{\partial x\,\partial y}&=\frac{m_1m_0(\theta_1-\theta_0)(m_1x-m_0y)}{D(x,y)^3}. \end{aligned}

The remainder is simpler in terms of a=m_1\Delta_1 and b=m_0\Delta_0, whose sum is a+b=\hat D-D. Substituting and collecting terms, the remainder factors:

R=\frac{\theta_1-\theta_0}{D(\tilde x,\tilde y)^3}\,(a+b)\,(m_1\tilde x\,b-m_0\tilde y\,a).

Because \tilde x and \tilde y are nonnegative, |m_1\tilde x\,b-m_0\tilde y\,a|\le(m_1\tilde x+m_0\tilde y)(|a|+|b|)=D(\tilde x,\tilde y)(|a|+|b|). So

|R|\le(\theta_1-\theta_0)\frac{|\hat D-D|\,(|a|+|b|)}{D(\tilde x,\tilde y)^2}.

Noticing where the curvature comes from

At this point you may notice something. Look at what the remainder is made of. Every term in it carries the factor a+b=\hat D-D, the error in the estimated number of votes cast. So all the curvature in h comes from its denominator. That’s because h is a ratio of two linear functions of the turnout rates: \hat\theta=\hat N/\hat D. This suggests a change of variables. Instead of expanding h in \hat\mu(1) and \hat\mu(0), work with \hat N and \hat D, and expand only the nonlinear piece, 1/\hat D, which is a function of one variable. The remainder told us what the change of variables should be. Noticing this makes the problem much easier, and it gives a tighter bound too. With practice, you’ll often see a change of variables like this before you expand anything, and you can skip straight to the next part. We’ll proceed as if you noticed it. If you didn’t, you can still get there by a more involved path, which we finish at the end.

Expanding only the denominator

Because N=\theta D, the error is a quotient whose numerator is linear in the turnout estimates.

\hat\theta-\theta=\frac{\hat N-\theta\hat D}{\hat D}.

All the nonlinearity is in 1/\hat D, so that’s what we expand, around u=D. For 1/u we can write the remainder exactly. You can check this identity by putting the right side over the common denominator D^2\hat D.

\frac{1}{\hat D}=\frac{1}{D}-\frac{\hat D-D}{D^2}+\frac{(\hat D-D)^2}{D^2\hat D}.

Multiplying by \hat N-\theta\hat D, the remainder factors. The 1/\hat D pairs with \hat N-\theta\hat D, and that ratio is our error again.

\frac{(\hat N-\theta\hat D)(\hat D-D)^2}{D^2\hat D} =\frac{\hat N-\theta\hat D}{\hat D}\cdot\frac{(\hat D-D)^2}{D^2} =(\hat\theta-\theta)\frac{(\hat D-D)^2}{D^2}.

So

\hat\theta-\theta =\frac{\hat N-\theta\hat D}{D} -\frac{(\hat N-\theta\hat D)(\hat D-D)}{D^2} +(\hat\theta-\theta)\frac{(\hat D-D)^2}{D^2}.

Now take expectations term by term. The first term is our linear approximation, c_1\Delta_1+c_0\Delta_0, and it has expectation zero. In the second term, \hat N-\theta\hat D=m_1(\theta_1-\theta)\Delta_1+m_0(\theta_0-\theta)\Delta_0 and \hat D-D=m_1\Delta_1+m_0\Delta_0. The two samples are independent, so the cross terms have expectation zero and

\operatorname{E}\left\{\frac{(\hat N-\theta\hat D)(\hat D-D)}{D^2}\right\} =\frac{m_1^2(\theta_1-\theta)V_1+m_0^2(\theta_0-\theta)V_0}{D^2} =\frac{c_1m_1V_1+c_0m_0V_0}{D}.

The last step uses \theta_1-\theta=m_0\mu(0)(\theta_1-\theta_0)/D and \theta_0-\theta=-m_1\mu(1)(\theta_1-\theta_0)/D. In the third term, both \hat\theta and \theta are averages of \theta_1 and \theta_0, so |\hat\theta-\theta|\le\theta_1-\theta_0. And \operatorname{E}(\hat D-D)^2=\operatorname{V}(\hat D)=m_1^2V_1+m_0^2V_0. Putting the three terms together gives our bound.

\left|\operatorname{E}\left(\hat\theta\right)-\theta\right| \le\frac{|c_1m_1V_1+c_0m_0V_0|}{D} +(\theta_1-\theta_0)\frac{\operatorname{V}(\hat D)}{D^2}.

Substituting c_1, c_0, D and V_w writes this in terms of m_1, m_0, \theta_1, \theta_0, \mu(1), \mu(0), n_1 and n_0. For the lecture’s design, with n_1=5{,}259 and n_0=12{,}270, the two terms are about 9.5\times10^{-7} and 1.7\times10^{-5}, so the bias is at most about 1.8\times10^{-5}. The estimator’s standard deviation from Exercise 23.3 is about s\approx0.00142, so the bias is at most about 1.2% of a standard deviation.

That is too small to worry about. If the estimator’s distribution is shifted by 0.012 standard deviations, the chance that it lands within 1.96 standard deviations of \theta falls from 95% to about 94.999%. Bias from the linear approximation won’t make the coverage of our interval much lower than 95%.

What this tells us

Our interval for the vote share is built on a linear approximation. This error analysis says that’s no reason to trust its coverage less than the coverage of an interval for something that is linear to begin with, like a mean or a difference in means. That’s a general approach worth remembering: use an approximation to reduce the problem you’re working on to one you understand, then check whether the approximation is a problem.

If you didn’t notice: finishing the direct expansion

Without the change of variables, we have to bound the remainder R directly. We don’t know where (\tilde x,\tilde y) is. D(\tilde x,\tilde y) lies somewhere between D and \hat D, and if \hat D were near zero this bound would be useless. So we split on whether \hat D\ge D/2.

When \hat D\ge D/2, we have D(\tilde x,\tilde y)\ge D/2, so |R|\le4(\theta_1-\theta_0)|\hat D-D|(|a|+|b|)/D^2. Because \operatorname{V}(\hat D)=\operatorname{E}(a^2)+\operatorname{E}(b^2), the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (|a|+|b|)^2\le2(a^2+b^2) give

\operatorname{E}\left\{|\hat D-D|\,(|a|+|b|)\right\} \le\sqrt{\operatorname{V}(\hat D)}\sqrt{2\operatorname{V}(\hat D)} =\sqrt2\,\operatorname{V}(\hat D).

When \hat D<D/2, we use cruder facts: |\hat\theta-\theta|\le\theta_1-\theta_0, so |R|\le(\theta_1-\theta_0)+|c_1\Delta_1+c_0\Delta_0|. This case is rare. By Chebyshev’s inequality, P(\hat D<D/2)\le P(|\hat D-D|>D/2)\le4\operatorname{V}(\hat D)/D^2. By Cauchy–Schwarz again, the linear term contributes at most s\sqrt{4\operatorname{V}(\hat D)/D^2}, where s is the estimator’s standard deviation from Exercise 23.3, about 0.00142.

Adding the two cases gives a bound.

\left|\operatorname{E}\left(\hat\theta\right)-\theta\right| \le(4\sqrt2+4)(\theta_1-\theta_0)\frac{\operatorname{V}(\hat D)}{D^2} +2s\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{V}(\hat D)}}{D}.

For the lecture’s design, this is about 1.8\times10^{-4}, or 12% of a standard deviation. That’s about ten times looser than the bound from expanding only the denominator, but it reaches the same conclusion: a shift of 0.12 standard deviations lowers coverage from 95% to about 94.8% at worst.

Sample Size and Allocation

Review: power for the turnout gap

The picture reviews the lecture’s Power Calculation. We estimated the turnout gap \delta=\mu(1)-\mu(0) using \hat\delta=\hat\mu(1)-\hat\mu(0). Using the lecture’s rounded rates, the old gap was \delta_0=0.600-0.682=-0.082. Our alternative was a two-point decrease, to \delta=0.580-0.682=-0.102. In lecture, we called a guess like that about the value of the estimand our alternative value, and designed our survey to meet our standard if that guess is right.

To be evidence of a decrease, an estimate’s right arm has to end to the left of the old gap. That arm is 1.65 standard errors long. The estimate therefore has to lie below the dashed cutoff.

\hat\delta+1.65\,\mathrm{se}<\delta_0 \quad\text{if and only if}\quad \hat\delta<\delta_0-1.65\,\mathrm{se}.

For 80% of estimates to meet this standard, the 80th percentile of the sampling distribution must be at or below the cutoff. That percentile is 0.84 standard errors above the alternative. The purple area is the proportion of estimates that meet the standard, and the two colored distances add to the separation between the alternative and the old gap.

\begin{aligned} \delta+0.84\,\mathrm{se}&\le\delta_0-1.65\,\mathrm{se} \\ \delta_0-\delta=0.02&\ge(1.65+0.84)\,\mathrm{se} \\ \mathrm{se}&\le\frac{0.02}{2.49}\approx0.008. \end{aligned}

In the lecture’s “How many calls?” exercise, proportional allocation needed 5,259 calls to Black voters and 12,270 to non-Black voters for this standard-error target. We’ll use that design as a benchmark for the cost of answering our new question.

The alternative world

We’ll keep the lecture’s hypothetical success of the Georgia Republican Party’s effort to suppress Black turnout and ask whether it costs the Democrats their majority. Using the lecture’s rounded rates, the original world has 60% Black turnout and 68.2% non-Black turnout; the alternative lowers Black turnout to 58%. With the hypothetical candidate preferences below, the implied statewide share moves from about 50.05% to 49.68%. These are the alternative population values we’ll use to design the survey.

\begin{alignedat}{2} \textcolor{#00BFC4}{\mu(1)} &= 0.580 &\quad& \text{turnout among Black voters, two points below 2020} \\ \textcolor{#F8766D}{\mu(0)} &= 0.682 &\quad& \text{turnout among non-Black voters, as in 2020} \\ \textcolor{#00BFC4}{m_1} &= 2{,}290{,}365 &\quad& \text{Black registered voters on Georgia's 2020 roll} \\ \textcolor{#F8766D}{m_0} &= 5{,}350{,}986 &\quad& \text{non-Black registered voters on that roll} \\ \theta_1 &= 0.90 &\quad& \text{Democratic vote share among Black voters} \\ \theta_0 &= 0.35 &\quad& \text{Democratic vote share among non-Black voters.} \end{alignedat}

For the election forecast, evidence of a lost majority means that a one-sided 95% confidence interval for the Democratic vote share lies entirely below one-half. We want that to happen in 80% of our samples if the reduced-turnout alternative is right. The Power Calculation in our lecture shows how to turn that requirement into a standard-error target. The direction is the same as in class: an upper confidence bound must fall below a reference value. Here the reference is one-half rather than the old turnout gap.

We will compare two ways to divide the calls. We call an allocation proportional if the groups receive the same shares of calls as their shares of registered voters. We call it equal if we call the same number of people in each group. Proportional allocation resembles the population; equal allocation gives the two turnout estimates the same number of observations. Our vote-share formula already uses the registration counts to account for the groups’ sizes, so the sample does not have to reproduce their proportions. The useful comparison is how much uncertainty each allocation leaves in the forecast, and how many calls it needs to meet the same power requirement.

Once we know how small the standard error must be, we can choose the numbers of calls n_1 and n_0 using the variance calculation from Exercise 23.3. The population turnout rates and candidate preferences stay fixed while we change those sample sizes. Statewide, each sample is a tiny fraction of its group’s roll, so (m_w-n_w)/(m_w-1) is close to one. For these design calculations we’ll replace those factors by one, as we did for the turnout gap in lecture, and check the resulting sample fractions. We’ll return to the full formula in McIntosh County.

SolutionSolution

The question and the picture. We want to know whether a turnout survey can show convincingly that the candidate has lost the majority. The power picture shows what that takes.

The technique. Our linear approximation to \hat\theta is a weighted combination of the two sample turnout rates: c_1\hat\mu(1)+c_0\hat\mu(0) plus a constant. So the lecture’s sample-size calculation for the turnout gap carries over, with each group’s variance \sigma^2(w) multiplied by c_w^2. It has two steps.

First, how small does the standard error have to be? The two distances in the picture have to fit between \theta and one-half: 0.84\,\mathrm{se} from the alternative to the cutoff and 1.65\,\mathrm{se} from the cutoff to one-half.

0.84\,\mathrm{se}+1.65\,\mathrm{se}\le0.5-\theta \quad\text{so}\quad \mathrm{se}\le\frac{0.5-\theta}{2.49}.

Second, how many calls does that take? Say a share f of the n calls goes to Black voters, so n_1=fn and n_0=(1-f)n. Then our variance formula from Exercise 23.3 is a factor that depends only on the split, divided by the number of calls. We leave out the finite-population factors, which are close to one when we call a small fraction of each roll.

\mathrm{se}^2\approx \textcolor{#00BFC4}{\frac{c_1^2\sigma^2(1)}{fn}} +\textcolor{#F8766D}{\frac{c_0^2\sigma^2(0)}{(1-f)n}} =\frac{g(f)}{n} \quad\text{where}\quad g(f)=\textcolor{#00BFC4}{\frac{c_1^2\sigma^2(1)}{f}}+\textcolor{#F8766D}{\frac{c_0^2\sigma^2(0)}{1-f}}.

Putting the two steps together gives the number of calls.

\frac{g(f)}{n}\le\left(\frac{0.5-\theta}{2.49}\right)^2 \quad\text{so}\quad n\ge g(f)\left(\frac{2.49}{0.5-\theta}\right)^2.

The allocation changes g(f) and nothing else. Parts 1 and 2 work out the first step for our alternative, and part 3 works out g(f) for two allocations.

  1. The estimator is now \hat\theta, and the reference value is 0.5. The vote share is the number of votes for the candidate divided by the number of votes cast. Substituting the population values gives

\begin{aligned} \theta &=\frac{m_1\mu(1)\theta_1+m_0\mu(0)\theta_0}{m_1\mu(1)+m_0\mu(0)} \\ &=\frac{2{,}290{,}365\times0.580\times0.90+5{,}350{,}986\times0.682\times0.35} {2{,}290{,}365\times0.580+5{,}350{,}986\times0.682} \\ &\approx 0.4967774. \end{aligned}

The candidate has about 49.68% of the votes, or 0.32 percentage points below one-half.

  1. The relabeled upper-bound event: a one-sided 95% interval lies below one-half when its upper endpoint \hat\theta+1.65\,\mathrm{se} does, that is, when

    \hat\theta<0.5-1.65\,\mathrm{se}.

    So the standard error has to be at most

\mathrm{se}=\frac{0.5-\theta}{2.49} \approx\frac{0.5-0.4967774}{2.49} \approx 0.00129420.

We’ll write s_\theta=(0.5-\theta)/2.49 for this standard-error target. It comes from how far the Democratic vote share is below one-half in our alternative world.

  1. Write c_1 and c_0 for the coefficients of the Black and non-Black turnout estimation errors in our linear approximation. Evaluating the derivatives from Exercise 23.2 at the population turnout rates gives c_1\approx0.185530 and c_0\approx-0.157782. Our variance formula, with the sampling fractions small enough to omit the finite-population factors, is

\mathrm{se}^2\approx \textcolor{#00BFC4}{\frac{c_1^2\sigma^2(1)}{n_1}} +\textcolor{#F8766D}{\frac{c_0^2\sigma^2(0)}{n_0}}.

Under proportional allocation, f=m_1/m: n_1=n\,m_1/m and n_0=n\,m_0/m, where n=n_1+n_0 and m=m_1+m_0. Substituting these sample sizes and factoring out 1/n gives g(f)/n for this split.

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{se}^2 &\approx\frac1n\left\{ \textcolor{#00BFC4}{\frac{c_1^2\sigma^2(1)}{m_1/m}} +\textcolor{#F8766D}{\frac{c_0^2\sigma^2(0)}{m_0/m}}\right\} \\ &\approx\frac1n\left\{ \textcolor{#00BFC4}{\frac{(0.185530)^2\times 0.580\times 0.420}{0.299733}} +\textcolor{#F8766D}{\frac{(-0.157782)^2\times 0.682\times 0.318}{0.700267}}\right\} \\ &\approx\frac{0.03568511}{n}. \end{aligned}

Solving the requirement \mathrm{se}^2\le s_\theta^2 for the total number of calls gives

n\ge\frac{0.03568511}{s_\theta^2} \approx\frac{0.03568511}{(0.00129420)^2} \approx 21305.16.

Rounding each group’s count up gives 6,386 calls to Black voters and 14,920 to non-Black voters: 21,306 calls, with approximate standard error 0.00129418.

Under equal allocation, f=1/2: n_1=n_0=n/2. Substitution into the same variance formula gives

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{se}^2 &\approx\frac{2}{n}\left\{\textcolor{#00BFC4}{c_1^2\sigma^2(1)}+\textcolor{#F8766D}{c_0^2\sigma^2(0)}\right\} \\ &\approx\frac{2}{n}\left\{ \textcolor{#00BFC4}{(0.185530)^2\times 0.580\times 0.420} +\textcolor{#F8766D}{(-0.157782)^2\times 0.682\times 0.318}\right\} \\ &\approx\frac{0.02756835}{n}. \end{aligned}

The resulting total requirement is

n\ge\frac{0.02756835}{s_\theta^2} \approx\frac{0.02756835}{(0.00129420)^2} \approx 16459.19.

Rounding each group’s count up gives 8,230 calls to each group: 16,460 calls, with approximate standard error 0.00129417. Every group sample in these two designs is less than 0.4% of its group’s roll, so the without-replacement factors are indeed close to one.

Proportional allocation needs 21,306 calls, about 22% more than the lecture’s 17,529; equal allocation needs 16,460, about 6% fewer. The vote share varies less from sample to sample because the turnout errors enter with coefficients smaller than one. But here we need to distinguish a share only 0.32 percentage points below one-half, rather than a turnout gap two points below its reference, and that much smaller distance requires a smaller standard error. The variance and the distance to detect both enter the call count, and changing the allocation changes the variance.

So the story is this: about 21,306 calls under proportional allocation, or 16,460 under equal allocation, give an 80% chance of convincing evidence that the majority is lost.

SolutionSolution

The question and the technique. In Exercise 24.1, the number of calls we need is g(f) times a number that doesn’t depend on the split. So the best split is the one that makes g(f) smallest. That’s the same as minimizing the variance with the total number of calls fixed, which is the lecture’s optimal allocation with each group’s variance \sigma^2(w) multiplied by c_w^2.

With n_0=n-n_1, the approximate variance is a function of the one sample size we can choose. Its derivative is

\frac{d}{dn_1}\left\{ \textcolor{#00BFC4}{\frac{c_1^2\sigma^2(1)}{n_1}} +\textcolor{#F8766D}{\frac{c_0^2\sigma^2(0)}{n-n_1}}\right\} =-\textcolor{#00BFC4}{\frac{c_1^2\sigma^2(1)}{n_1^2}} +\textcolor{#F8766D}{\frac{c_0^2\sigma^2(0)}{(n-n_1)^2}}.

Setting this derivative to zero equates the two positive terms. Taking positive square roots and using n_0=n-n_1 gives the allocation ratio.

\begin{aligned} \frac{c_0^2\sigma^2(0)}{n_0^2} &=\frac{c_1^2\sigma^2(1)}{n_1^2} \\ \frac{|c_0|\sigma(0)}{n_0} &=\frac{|c_1|\sigma(1)}{n_1} \\ \frac{n_1}{n_0} &=\frac{|c_1|\sigma(1)}{|c_0|\sigma(0)}. \end{aligned}

The second derivative is positive for 0<n_1<n, so this is the minimum.

\frac{2c_1^2\sigma^2(1)}{n_1^3} +\frac{2c_0^2\sigma^2(0)}{(n-n_1)^3}>0.

Writing s_1=|c_1|\sigma(1) and s_0=|c_0|\sigma(0) makes the two sample sizes and their total easy to relate. The optimal sizes are

n_1=n\frac{s_1}{s_1+s_0} \quad\text{and}\quad n_0=n\frac{s_0}{s_1+s_0}.

Substitution shows the variance at that allocation.

\mathrm{se}^2\approx \frac{s_1^2}{n s_1/(s_1+s_0)} +\frac{s_0^2}{n s_0/(s_1+s_0)} =\frac{(s_1+s_0)^2}{n}.

This is the lecture’s result with each group’s standard deviation multiplied by the magnitude of its coefficient in the vote-share approximation. The numerical values are

\begin{aligned} s_1&\approx 0.185530\sqrt{0.580\times0.420} \approx 0.091570, \\ \quad\text{and}\quad s_0&\approx 0.157782\sqrt{0.682\times0.318} \approx 0.073479. \end{aligned}

Thus about 55.48% of the calls go to Black voters. At this split g(f)=(s_1+s_0)^2, so the standard error is (s_1+s_0)/\sqrt n and the requirement \mathrm{se}\le s_\theta from Exercise 24.1 holds when

n\ge\frac{(s_1+s_0)^2}{s_\theta^2} \approx\frac{(0.091570+0.073479)^2}{(0.00129420)^2} \approx 16263.80.

The smallest possible integer total is therefore 16,264. At that total, checking the two integers on either side of the continuous optimum gives 9,023 Black and 7,241 non-Black calls. Their approximate standard error is 0.00129419, below the required 0.00129420. We save 5042 calls compared with proportional allocation, and 196 compared with equal allocation.

McIntosh County

Statewide, the correction factors are close to one, so we dropped them. McIntosh County is much smaller: in 2024, its roll had m_1 = 2{,}729 Black and m_0 = 7{,}777 non-Black registered voters, m = 10{,}506 in all. We will choose the allocation and total number of calls again, using this smaller roll and retaining the without-replacement factors.

Each dot below represents one registered voter. The positions illustrate the counts, not actual residential locations.

The maps show why calling a substantial fraction of this roll is quite different from calling a tiny fraction of the state. Sampling without replacement reduces uncertainty as we cover more of each group, and it leaves none when we have called everyone. The factors you derived in Homework 4 describe that change.

The new algebra comes from those correction factors. I’ll expand one group’s contribution so that you can reuse the optimization in Exercise 24.2. Distributing the numerator separates the part that depends on the number called from a fixed population term.

\begin{aligned} \frac{\sigma^2(w)}{n_w}\frac{m_w-n_w}{m_w-1} &=\frac{\sigma^2(w)m_w}{n_w(m_w-1)} -\frac{\sigma^2(w)n_w}{n_w(m_w-1)} \\ &=\frac{m_w}{m_w-1}\frac{\sigma^2(w)}{n_w} -\frac{\sigma^2(w)}{m_w-1}. \end{aligned}

For the turnout gap, we add these contributions from the two groups. For the vote-share approximation, we first multiply each group’s contribution by its squared coefficient, c_w^2. We write V(n_1,n_0) for the resulting variance expression. Collecting the fixed population quantities into constants A_1, A_0 and C gives the form

V(n_1,n_0)=\frac{A_1}{n_1}+\frac{A_0}{n_0}-C.

The constants differ between the two questions. They stay fixed while we choose the call counts. This expansion is the supplied step; you’ll identify the constants, minimize the variance at a fixed total, and find the total needed for the relevant power target. The normal-approximation power argument stays the same. Its standard error must now come from the full without-replacement variance. Finally, a design without replacement must have n_1\le m_1 and n_0\le m_0: we cannot call more distinct people than a group contains.

Without replacement, we multiply each group’s contribution in the expansion by its squared coefficient. The variance is again the with-replacement variance, to within the same factors m_w/(m_w-1), moved down by a constant C. So the same optimization and size calculation apply.

\begin{aligned} A_1&=c_1^2\frac{m_1}{m_1-1}\sigma^2(1) \approx (0.167856)^2\frac{2{,}729}{2{,}728}\times0.2436 \approx 0.00686608, \\ A_0&=c_0^2\frac{m_0}{m_0-1}\sigma^2(0) \approx (-0.142751)^2\frac{7{,}777}{7{,}776}\times0.216876 \approx 0.00442005, \\ C&=\frac{c_1^2\sigma^2(1)}{m_1-1}+\frac{c_0^2\sigma^2(0)}{m_0-1} \approx\frac{(0.167856)^2\times0.2436}{2{,}728} +\frac{(-0.142751)^2\times0.216876}{7{,}776} \approx 0.0000030843. \end{aligned}

Here K=(\sqrt{A_1}+\sqrt{A_0})^2\approx 0.02230399, and about 55.48% of calls go to Black voters, against 55.48% with replacement. This time C is only about 3% of the target variance s_\theta^2, so raising the target by C barely changes it. The required total is

n\ge\frac{K}{s_\theta^2+C} \approx\frac{0.02230399}{(0.00947396)^2+0.0000030843} \approx 240.24.

The smallest sufficient integer design has 134 Black and 107 non-Black calls: 241 calls in total. Substituting them into the full vote-share variance, with each group’s contribution multiplied by its squared coefficient and its without-replacement factor, verifies the precision requirement.

\mathrm{se} =\sqrt{(0.167856)^2\frac{0.2436}{134}\frac{2{,}595}{2{,}728} +(-0.142751)^2\frac{0.216876}{107}\frac{7{,}670}{7{,}776}} \approx 0.00945854<0.00947396.

Both counts fit within the county’s rolls. They sample 4.91% and 1.38% of the groups, respectively. Including the correction saves only 8 calls here.

That’s the intuition. The constant C is its arithmetic.

The county has a smaller share of Black registrants, the group that supports this candidate more strongly. With the same group turnout and candidate preferences, its Democratic vote share is farther below one-half: about 47.64%, compared with 49.68% statewide. That larger distance allows a larger standard error and fewer calls. In this county, the candidate was already below one-half at the earlier turnout rates (about 47.97%). The county survey tests whether the candidate is below one-half under the reduced-turnout alternative; it does not establish a local majority reversal.

To compare the two county questions, first use their optimal designs with replacement. Each call count is its minimized variance factor divided by its own squared standard-error target, so their ratio has two factors.

\begin{aligned} \frac{\text{vote-share calls}}{\text{turnout-gap calls}} &=\frac{\{|c_1|\sigma(1)+|c_0|\sigma(0)\}^2}{\{\sigma(1)+\sigma(0)\}^2} \left(\frac{0.008}{s_\theta}\right)^2 \\ &\approx\frac{0.02229818}{0.92017587} \left(\frac{0.008}{0.00947396}\right)^2 \\ &\approx 0.0242\times0.71 \\ &\approx 0.017. \end{aligned}

The vote share’s variance factor is smaller because each turnout error is multiplied by a coefficient whose magnitude is below one. Its standard-error target is also larger than the turnout gap’s 0.008, because its distance below one-half is larger than two percentage points. Both differences reduce the number of calls needed for the vote share. Including the finite-population factors reduces the optimal totals to 241 for the vote share and 5,080 for the turnout gap.

Sampling without replacement moves each question’s variance down by its own constant C, so it raises the target variance from s^2 to s^2+C. That matters when C is large compared with s^2. The rolls set C, but the question sets s^2. For the turnout gap, C is about 1.8 times 0.008^2, and the calls fall to about 35% of the with-replacement total. For the vote share, C is about 3% of s_\theta^2, and the calls fall by about 3%. The question we want the survey to answer determines how much of the population we need to call.
