Summary

How you design a study is as important or more important than how you analyze it. If you screw up the analysis, you can just analyze it again. But if you screw up the design, you’ve run a study that you can’t use to make your point. This happens pretty often. The National Supported Work experiment is an example.

How you design a study is not just relevant to the people who are running the study. It tells you something very important about how you analyze it, because an underpowered study, if it gives you a conclusive-seeming answer, is pretty likely to be giving you the wrong conclusions.

We’ll work through some questions on study design and its implications for data analysis, then turn our attention to your final project, where we’ll have to watch out for this issue among others. There are a variety of ways to report essentially the same statistical result in use, so we’ll prepare with some exercises on translating those back into the terms we use in this class.

Was NSW Big Enough?

In Tuesday’s lecture, we estimated the effect of National Supported Work (NSW) job training on 1978 earnings using 185 trained men and 260 controls. Our estimate was about $1,794, with a conservative 95% confidence interval from $479 to $3,109 in 1978 dollars. A dollar in 1978 was worth about what $4.80 is today.

\begin{aligned} 1{,}794 \pm 1{,}315 &\approx [479,\ 3{,}109] &&\quad\text{is a 95\% confidence interval in 1978 dollars}\quad \\ 8{,}613 \pm 6{,}313 &\approx [2{,}300,\ 14{,}926] &&\quad\text{adjusted for inflation}\quad \end{aligned}

So it’s between about $500 and $3,100 a year. That’s a big range, from irrelevant to incredibly worth it. This was an expensive program to run. If the average effect were at the low end of the interval, it wouldn’t be cost-effective. If it were at the high end of the interval, it would be. To figure out if it’s worth running, we need a more conclusive result.

That was probably a disappointment to the people running the program. They probably wanted to show that it was effective. Not knowing is, frankly, probably worse than knowing that it wasn’t a successful program. Let’s think about what went into planning.

Imagine we’re planning NSW. The study hasn’t run, so we don’t know what it will find. We’ve been offered funding for the program if we can demonstrate that its average gain exceeds $1,000, and we think the gain is about $2,000. To do our power analysis, we need to know how much earnings vary. For the controls we use the CPS peer group from Tuesday’s lecture: in 1978 they earned about $7,103 on average, with a spread of about $7,102. We’re optimistic about the program, and larger earnings can be more variable, so we take the trained men’s spread to grow in proportion to their earnings, to about $9,101. With 185 trained men and 260 controls, Tuesday’s conservative standard-error bound is about $801.

\mathrm{se} \le \sqrt{\frac{\textcolor{#00BFC4}{\sigma^2(1)}}{m_1} + \frac{\textcolor{#F8766D}{\sigma^2(0)}}{m_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{\textcolor{#00BFC4}{9{,}101^2}}{185} + \frac{\textcolor{#F8766D}{7{,}102^2}}{260}} \approx \$801.

People usually say that our alternative is $2,000. Really, the alternative is a description of a sampling distribution. Here, that sampling distribution is located at our nominal alternative, $2,000, with a standard error of $801.2

As in the turnout-gap power review in Homework 6, 80% power with a one-sided 95% interval needs the assumed effect to sit 1.65 + 0.84 = 2.49 standard errors above the standard. With NSW’s 445 participants, the $2,000 effect is only 1.25 standard errors above the standard.

\frac{\$2{,}000 - \$1{,}000}{\$801} \approx 1.25.

The top plot below shows the fraction of one-sided 95% intervals, drawn from the sampling distribution our alternative describes, that lie strictly above the standard. It’s about 35%. That’s our power.

What happened is that the estimate \hat\tau came out at $1,794, about the same as our alternative—our guess at the actual average treatment effect \tau—and it settled nothing. Even though this interval is narrower than the plan said it would be, it still crosses the $1,000 standard. We got roughly the effect we hoped for and couldn’t show it. That shouldn’t be a surprise. If we were to design for 35% power perfectly, this would happen 65 percent of the time.

Sizing the Study

How many men would give us 80% power? We’ll increase the numbers of trained men and controls by the same factor k, keeping the within-group income variances we used in our plan. Multiplying both group sizes by k divides the standard-error bound by \sqrt{k}. For 80% power, our alternative must be at least 2.49 standard errors above the funding standard.

\mathrm{se} \le \frac{\text{assumed effect} - \text{standard}}{2.49} = \frac{\$2{,}000 - \$1{,}000}{2.49} \approx \$402.

The standard-error bound must fall from about $801 to $402. This determines the factor k.

k \ge \left(\frac{\text{original standard-error bound}}{\text{required standard-error bound}}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{801}{402}\right)^2 \approx 3.97.

Multiplying the original 445 men by this factor and rounding up gives 1,766 men: 734 trained and 1,032 controls. And our power diagram looks like this.

What if controls were free?

Could we get 80% power without training more men? Controls are way cheaper, because you don’t have to train them. Suppose, as an extreme case, that controls are free. We keep the 185 trained men and vary the number of controls, m_0. Using the same within-group income variances as before gives us a bound on the standard error.

\mathrm{se}(m_0)\le\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2(1)}{185}+\frac{\sigma^2(0)}{m_0}}\approx\sqrt{\frac{9{,}101^2}{185}+\frac{7{,}102^2}{m_0}}\text{ dollars}.

Allocating a Fixed Budget

In practice, controls cost less than treated participants, but both cost something. Suppose training and following one participant costs c_1 = \$4{,}000 and following one control costs c_0 = \$1{,}000. At those prices, NSW’s 185 trained men and 260 controls cost B = \$1{,}000{,}000. That’s our budget. With a fixed budget, we have to choose how many people to put in each group, so we ask whether the same money could have been split better. In terms of m_0 and m_1, we get this bound on the standard error.

\mathrm{se}(m_0,m_1)\le\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2(1)}{m_1}+\frac{\sigma^2(0)}{m_0}}\approx\sqrt{\frac{9{,}101^2}{m_1}+\frac{7{,}102^2}{m_0}}\text{ dollars}.

We’re going to do something a lot like what we did in our Georgia turnout example. In that case, we had two group sizes as well, but we knew the total number of calls we were willing to make. Once we chose how many people to call in one group, that fixed the number in the other.

n_0+n_1=n \qquad\text{so}\qquad n_0=n-n_1.

Here, we know how much we’re willing to spend. Because every participant costs a multiple of $1,000, spending less than B leaves enough for at least one more control, so the best split spends the full budget. That gives us an equation relating the two group sizes, so choosing how many men to train fixes how many controls we can afford.

c_0m_0+c_1m_1=B \qquad\text{so}\qquad m_0=\frac{B-c_1m_1}{c_0}.

As in Georgia, we can describe the split with a fraction f. Here, f is the fraction of the budget spent on trained men.

f=\frac{c_1m_1}{B} \qquad\text{so}\qquad m_1=\frac{fB}{c_1} \qquad\text{and}\qquad m_0=\frac{(1-f)B}{c_0}.

The group sizes must be integers. With our costs and budget, we can spend the full budget by training any whole number of men from 0 to 250 and spending the rest on controls. These choices give the possible budget shares.

f=\frac{c_1m_1}{B}=\frac{4{,}000m_1}{1{,}000{,}000}=\frac{m_1}{250}\in\left\{\frac{0}{250},\frac{1}{250},\ldots,\frac{250}{250}\right\}.

The same integer constraint arose in Georgia, though we didn’t mention it there. What we did then, and will do now, is a common optimization technique called relaxation. We drop that constraint and minimize over the whole interval [0,1] using calculus.

What happens among the significant results?

Often papers ask whether an estimate differs significantly from zero in either direction. A result is called significant when its two-sided 95% confidence interval excludes zero. What happens to the size and sign of the estimates when we select only significant results?

We’ll set aside the details of the study design and work with normal sampling distributions centered at a true effect of 2,000. We vary the standard error to compare studies with 10%, 50%, and 80% power. For the two-sided interval, the power calculation uses 2.80 in place of 2.49, and our standard is zero. For 80% power, we need the following standard error.

\mathrm{se}\le\frac{\text{assumed effect}-\text{standard}}{2.80}=\frac{2{,}000-0}{2.80}\approx 714.

The standard errors for 10%, 50%, and 80% power are approximately 3,066, 1,020, and 714.

In the top picture, an estimate close to the true effect of 2,000 cannot be called significant. To exclude zero, its magnitude has to exceed 6,009—more than three times the true effect. If we look only at significant results, we discard estimates close to the truth and keep estimates that make the effect look much larger than it is.

Gelman and Carlin (2014) call this Type M (magnitude) error. We measure the exaggeration by dividing the average magnitude among significant estimates by the magnitude of the true effect.3 As we move down the pictures, the intervals get narrower, and estimates closer to the true effect can be significant. We keep more of those estimates, so the average magnitude among significant results moves closer to the truth.

Occasionally, an estimate goes so far in the other direction that its entire interval lies below zero. That study would report evidence of a negative effect, even though the true effect is positive. They call a significant estimate with the wrong sign a Type S (sign) error. The Type S error rate is the fraction of significant estimates with the wrong sign.4

Now imagine that journals publish only the significant results. Readers would see the exaggerated effects and occasionally a result pointing in the wrong direction, while the estimates closer to the truth in the low-power study would go unseen. This is an example of publication bias: whether a study gets published depends on its result. The estimates can come from correctly conducted studies; the selection changes the picture readers get.

Selection can also happen within a study. An analyst might try several outcomes or subgroups and report only the most favorable significant result. This is p-hacking. It gives the analyst several chances to get a significant result, but the reported interval does not account for that search. Neither publication bias nor p-hacking requires anyone to fabricate data, and good intentions do not prevent either problem.

This can be helpful in understanding the papers you’ll read for your final project, in particular their context and motivation. Maybe a paper investigates a compelling-sounding effect that failed to replicate. Maybe it’s a small study reporting a surprisingly big effect.

Final Project: The Skeleton of a Paper

For your final project, you’ll dissect at least two papers. You choose them, and you’ll agree on them with me before submitting this homework. This week, you’ll give a full citation and a working link for each one and write the skeleton of one of them. The second paper comes next week, when we’ll also go a couple of levels deeper and put things in the language of the class.

A paper doesn’t just report an analysis. It’s written to convince you that its authors’ conclusions are true. A skeleton takes that away, so you can see what the paper claims and what holds those claims up. The rest of the writing isn’t necessarily unimportant, but with the skeleton in hand, you can look at it for what it is. The skeleton is the outer level of an outline of the paper, an outline of its concepts rather than its writing. It’s the who, what, when and where of the statistics. What are the authors estimating? What’s their estimator? What’s their statistical claim? What do they conclude about the world? How much of that follows directly from their analysis, and what sits in between?

Read Andrew Gelman’s post “How to read (in quantitative social science). And by implication, how to write.” The post isn’t a detailed how-to, and we’ll give you more structure than it does. But it’s a good supplement. Gelman is asking whether a paper’s evidence meets his standards, both as statistical evidence and in how it fits the story the paper tells. Part of that is whether the paper gives you, the reader, enough to form your own opinion, or whether you’d have to take the authors’ word for it or redo the analysis yourself. To really understand a paper, you want some level of access to the data. Maybe the right graph or two is enough. But if the ones you want aren’t in the paper, you’re out of luck. That’s why, partway through the tables, he says he’s “desperate for a graph.” He’s writing for readers a little further along than you are. The comments sometimes have useful lessons too, but they’re blog comments, so read them thoughtfully.

The skeleton asks for each result as an interval estimate, and papers often report tests or p-values instead. So before we get to it, we’ll take a detour on how to read those as intervals.

Reading Tests and p-Values

A test and a p-value are both statements about intervals. A test tells us whether an interval contains a particular value, like zero. A p-value tells us at which levels it does. We’ll take tests first, then p-values.

Tests

A test answers the question of whether the estimand could be some specific value. When its answer is no, we say it rejects that value, or the null hypothesis that the estimand is that value.5

Let’s start with the simplest case. Suppose we have an estimate \hat\theta, we know its standard error \mathrm{se}, and we ask whether the estimand could be some particular value \theta_0. With the normal approximation we’ve been using, the test rejects \theta_0 if the estimate is more than 1.96 standard errors away from it. This is transparently equivalent to saying that \theta_0 is not in the usual \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{se} confidence interval. We reject if

\frac{|\hat\theta - \theta_0|}{\mathrm{se}} > 1.96 \quad \text{i.e. if} \quad \theta_0 \notin [\hat\theta - 1.96\,\mathrm{se},\ \hat\theta + 1.96\,\mathrm{se}].

This is called a two-sided z test at the 5% level. The ratio on the left is its z-statistic. Its level is the frequency it’s allowed to reject the value that’s actually true. And we say it’s valid if it rejects the true value no more frequently, and calibrated if it rejects it with exactly that frequency. For whatever reason, we talk about coverage probability when we talk about interval estimates and non-coverage probability when we talk about tests. For the sake of simplicity, we have assumed that we know the standard error. When it’s estimated, i.e. always, this is inexact. What a paper reports is an estimated standard error, and the ratio you get by dividing by that is called a t-statistic. And it’s common practice to use a sample-size-dependent cutoff that is slightly larger than 1.96, which is called a t test. That does give you exact coverage in a stylized example and tends to be a bit closer generally—especially in small samples.

This works for any test. We call the set of values a test doesn’t reject its confidence region. It contains the true value exactly when the test doesn’t reject the true value, so a test with level 5% gives a confidence region with 95% coverage. It works in the other direction too. If we have a 95% confidence region, rejecting the values outside it is a test with level 5%. For the two-sided z test, it’s an interval, as we’ve just seen. For a one-sided z test, which rejects \theta_0 if the estimate is more than 1.65 standard errors above it, we get our usual one-sided confidence interval. For some other tests, it can come in separate pieces. People usually don’t like that, because it’s hard to think of as a range of plausible values. So when a paper reports a test, it’s worth thinking about which test it is and what kind of confidence region it gives. If you’re not sure, it’s worth looking up, and you can always talk to me.

Terminology. A few more words you’ll see in papers.

  • Type I error: rejecting the value that’s actually true. A test’s level is the frequency it’s allowed to do this.
  • Type II error: not rejecting a value that’s false. A test’s power is one minus the frequency that it does this.

We’ve been talking about the power of confidence intervals. But typically, people talk about power in the context of tests. What we’ve been doing, in more conventional language, is talking about the power of the tests equivalent to our confidence intervals.

Tests throw away a lot of information. You take a nice interval estimate that you can draw and visualize, and then you boil it down by drawing a vertical line at an arbitrarily chosen position and saying, “Is it here?” You’ve thrown away an entire dimension of information. It’s wasteful. If you want to get a confidence region back from a test, you have to draw the line absolutely everywhere. In short, a confidence interval isn’t really equivalent to a test. It’s equivalent to a family of tests, one for each value of \theta_0.

p-Values

Nobody reports a test, ever. What they do report is a p-value. A p-value describes a family of tests that is, in my opinion and that of many others, less useful. It’s the family of tests you get for a single value of \theta_0, at varying levels. Usually that value is zero. The p-value is the smallest level at which the test rejects it. In terms of intervals, 1-p is the level of the interval that just touches zero. Intervals at lower levels exclude zero, and intervals at higher levels contain it.

Suppose again that we know the standard error. A paper reports \hat\theta = 0.12, and the standard error is 0.05. Then the purple curve is the estimated sampling distribution, and the intervals get wider as we go down. The pink one, at a level of about 98.4\%, just touches zero. The 95% interval excludes zero, so the test at the 5% level rejects zero. The 99.5% interval at the bottom includes zero, so the test at the 0.5% level doesn’t. The yellow tails are the mass the pink interval leaves out, about 0.016. That’s the two-sided p-value. The tails are hard to see because there isn’t much there.

Again, you don’t know this, which is why you never hear the term z-statistic or z test. What the paper reports is an estimated standard error, and the ratio 0.12/0.05 = 2.4 is its t-statistic for the test of \theta_0 = 0. Here is the same picture drawn with a t distribution, for a sample of size 12. The tails are heavier, so the interval that just touches zero has a lower level, about 96.5\%, and the p-value is bigger, about 0.035.

The stars you’ll often see beside estimates in a paper’s tables say how small the p-value is. R’s model summaries, for example, use one star for p < 0.05, two for p < 0.01, and three for p < 0.001. With the normal approximation, each one is a statement about an interval we know.

Reported result Interval that excludes zero Distance of the estimate from zero
p < 0.05 (*) Two-sided 95% interval More than 1.96 standard errors
p < 0.01 (**) Two-sided 99% interval More than 2.58 standard errors
p < 0.001 (***) Two-sided 99.9% interval More than 3.29 standard errors

So the stars run from about two standard errors to a little over three. Conventions vary, though, so it’s worth looking in the small print for what thresholds a paper’s stars stand for.

The Skeleton

For your first paper, you’ll write a skeleton with five parts. For each statistical and substantive claim, you’ll find the place where the authors make it, quote it, and then translate it into the language of the class.

  1. What kind of paper is it? Does the paper build its argument around one main finding, or does it assemble many findings to describe a subject? Gelman distinguishes these two kinds of paper. Where does yours fall, and is there another description that fits it better?
  2. Motivation. What is happening in the world, and what is already understood in the literature? Why are the authors asking this question now?
  3. Statistical claims. For each one:
    • What is the exact quote?
    • What is the estimand? What population is studied, what outcome is measured, in what units, and when?
    • What is the estimator? Sometimes the paper phrases it as a test.
    • What is the statistical claim? Is it an interval estimate, a p-value, something else? Do they provide a point estimate as well?
    • Can you express their statistical result as an interval estimate?
    • Does this statistical claim correspond to a causal claim? If it does, which treatment is compared with what, and for whom? Is that correspondence justified? Justifying it is called identification, and we’ll talk about it on Tuesday.
  4. Substantive claims. For each one:
    • What is the exact quote?
    • Is it a conclusion about the world, a claim about impact, or a stated or tacit recommendation about policy or behavior?
    • What sits between this conclusion and the statistical claims?
    • How directly does it follow from the analysis, and what else would it need?

Appendix: How conservative is the bound? (Optional)

This appendix is very optional. It’s here for anyone curious about a quirk we mentioned in a footnote, and nothing else in the homework depends on it. It’s also rougher than the rest of the homework. If you got here by reading straight through, you’re done.

In Was NSW Big Enough? we said that $801 is a bit larger than the largest standard error we can get when \sigma(1) and \sigma(0) equal $9,101 and $7,102, which is about $795. Here is where $795 comes from. Everything we need is in Tuesday’s lecture.

The formula. Each man’s individual effect is \tau_j = y_j(1) - y_j(0), and \sigma^2(\tau) is the variance of those effects across the m participants. Tuesday’s variance formula expresses \operatorname{Var}(\hat\tau) in terms of \sigma^2(1), \sigma^2(0), and \sigma^2(\tau).

\operatorname{Var}(\hat\tau) = \frac{m}{m-1}\left\{\frac{\sigma^2(1)}{m_1} + \frac{\sigma^2(0)}{m_0} - \frac{\sigma^2(\tau)}{m}\right\}.

Here the factor m/(m-1) is 445/444, which changes the standard errors below by less than a dollar, so we leave it out, as we did in the main text. The conservative bound drops the last term, \sigma^2(\tau)/m. So \operatorname{Var}(\hat\tau) is largest when the individual effects vary as little as they can.

The effects have to vary. If the effect were constant, every man’s trained earnings would be his untrained earnings plus the same amount. Adding a constant doesn’t change the standard deviation of earnings, so \sigma(1) and \sigma(0) would be equal. In our plan they are about $9,101 and $7,102. So \sigma(\tau) isn’t zero, and we can’t reach the $801 bound.

How little can they vary? We can find the smallest possible \sigma(\tau) by picturing the centered outcomes as two arrows. The centered outcomes z_j(1) = y_j(1) - \mu(1) and z_j(0) = y_j(0) - \mu(0) are the coordinates of two arrows, z(1) and z(0). Their squared lengths are \|z(1)\|^2 = m\,\sigma^2(1) and \|z(0)\|^2 = m\,\sigma^2(0). Their difference has coordinates \tau_j - \bar\tau, so its squared length is \|z(1) - z(0)\|^2 = m\,\sigma^2(\tau). With \theta for the angle between the arrows, the cross-term sum is \sum_{j=1}^m z_j(1)z_j(0) = \|z(1)\|\,\|z(0)\|\cos\theta, which expands the squared length of the difference arrow.

\|z(1) - z(0)\|^2 = \|z(1)\|^2 + \|z(0)\|^2 - 2\,\|z(1)\|\,\|z(0)\|\cos\theta.

For fixed arrow lengths, the difference is longest when the arrows point in opposite directions (\cos\theta = -1). At the other end, \cos\theta \le 1 makes the difference shortest when they point in the same direction (\cos\theta = 1).

\|z(1) - z(0)\|^2 \ge \|z(1)\|^2 + \|z(0)\|^2 - 2\,\|z(1)\|\,\|z(0)\| = \bigl(\|z(1)\| - \|z(0)\|\bigr)^2.

Dividing both sides by m and taking square roots bounds \sigma(\tau) from below.

\sigma(\tau) \ge \sigma(1) - \sigma(0) \approx \$9{,}101 - \$7{,}102 = \$1{,}999.

The largest standard error. Substituting the smallest possible \sigma(\tau) into the variance formula gives the sharpest upper bound on the standard error.

\mathrm{se} \le \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2(1)}{m_1} + \frac{\sigma^2(0)}{m_0} - \frac{\{\sigma(1) - \sigma(0)\}^2}{m}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{9{,}101^2}{185} + \frac{7{,}102^2}{260} - \frac{1{,}999^2}{445}} \approx \$795.

When do we get it? The bound of $795 holds with equality when the two arrows point in the same direction, meaning z_j(1) = 1.28\, z_j(0) for every man. Each man’s trained earnings then sit 28% farther from the group average than his untrained earnings do. That happens if training raises every man’s earnings by that same proportion, consistent with how we scaled \sigma(1) in our plan. In that case the standard error is about $795, and $801 is the conservative bound on it.
