National Supported Work was a 1970s employment program for people facing serious barriers to work, including former drug users, former offenders, and young school dropouts.

Our dataset contains 445 men, of whom 185 were assigned treatment and 260 control.

These weren’t randomly selected American men. They were recruited into a program intended to help people struggling to find work, often beyond the difficulties associated with their demographic circumstances.

Our target is these 445 participants’ mean earnings under the program, including the program earnings we don’t observe for the controls.

The 445 are a selected analysis sample from the larger NSW experiment, not the entire original experiment.

Change the Conventions

  • Back to two-sided intervals. We can demand higher interval coverage, or a higher chance of detecting success.
  • People describe these as two requirements. In the picture, they add up to one distance in standard errors.

Power From Bias

What people don’t talk about enough is the power you get from bias. Not like, metaphorically.

  • First, imagine a biased estimator of the same mean. Keep green fixed. Move blue, the center of repeated estimates, toward orange without changing interval widths.
  • The procedure does not know which line is true. It only knows the distance from blue to the line we ask about.
  • Then shrink the intervals with the bias held fixed. Precision helps us rule out the truth. Coverage is just lack of two-sided power to reject the truth.

\underset{\text{coverage}}{\Pr(\text{covers green})} \;=\; 1-\underset{\text{power}}{\Pr(\text{doesn't cover green})}

Green stays put. Blue moves; the interval widths stay the same.

n=501: 80% exclude green; 20% cover green.

n=1002: 98% exclude green; 2% cover green.

n=2004: 99.99% exclude green; 0.01% cover green.

Reality and What We’re Doing

  • What we have is a point estimate and an estimated sampling distribution that tells us a width.
  • Green is something we want to know, and don’t see. An interval says: we think green is somewhere in here.
  • With an unbiased estimator, we have a theoretical reason to center that interval where we do. Bias can take that reason away.

Real Data, Fake Data

To check coverage, we need to know the truth. We don’t know the real untreated mean, so we’ll construct a world where we do.

  • For this exercise, declare the observed NSW controls to be the whole target population. Green is its known mean: 4,555 dollars.
  • Is this legit?
    • Yes—as an experiment with a known target and real-looking populations.
    • It does not establish coverage of the real NSW population’s untreated mean. If it did, we wouldn’t have a precision problem to start with.

Make It Bigger

One copy: 286 records. Our observed CPS subgroup.

Two copies: 572 records. Twice as many records, the same earnings distribution.

Four copies: 1144 records. More records again; the mean still hasn’t moved.

  • We also have to synthesize the huge population that supplies the CPS subgroup.
  • Take k copies of the observed subgroup. More people, same earnings distribution, same mean.
  • Hold the study size fixed and let k grow. Sampling without replacement approaches sampling with replacement from the observed records.
  • We’ll use that limiting model. Then we can make the study as large as we like without changing the population we sample.

Calculating Coverage

Set Up

  • Return to n=30. Above, I’ve drawn the sampling distribution for the estimator \hat\mu and a normal approximation to it.
    • The blue line is its center, \tilde\mu = 7,103. The green line is the truth, \mu = 4,555.
  • What coverage should we expect at n=30? We’ll calculate an approximation.
  • Treat the normal approximation as exact, and use the estimator’s actual standard error instead of estimating it.
  • Here \sigma means the standard error of \hat\mu, not the spread of individual earnings. Our intervals are \hat\mu\pm1.96\sigma.

Which Estimates Hit The Target?

Let’s think about things from the target’s perspective. The solid green line.

Imagine giving green arms the same length as our intervals have.

Which point estimates produce intervals that hit the target?

Which estimates hit green? Those between its dotted arms.

An interval’s arms reach the target if and only if the target’s arms reach that interval’s center.

We integrate the sampling distribution’s density between the target’s arms.

\text{coverage} = \int_{\mu-1.96\sigma}^{\mu+1.96\sigma} f(x) dx \qqtext{ where } f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \sigma} e^{-\frac{(x-\tilde\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}

Ask for the region, then shade it and continue. This is a spoken question while working through the picture, not a request for students to write an answer at their desks. The normal density formula is supplied; they do not need to recall it.

Everything in Standard Errors

Earnings units. Green is 4,555 dollars; blue is 7,103 dollars.

Standard-error units. Blue is zero; green is -1.97. One unit is 1,294 dollars.

u=\frac{x-\tilde\mu}{\sigma},\qquad \text{green is at }-\frac{\tilde\mu-\mu}{\sigma} =-\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}}.

Same picture. Same shaded probability. A different ruler.

Five Minutes

Rewrite this probability in standard-error units:

\int_{\mu-1.96\sigma}^{\mu+1.96\sigma} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{-\frac{(x-\tilde\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\,dx.

Use u=(x-\tilde\mu)/\sigma. Write down the new endpoints, the new exponent, and what happens to dx.

It’s conceptually simple and a little finicky. Try it; then we’ll look at the solution.

A Standard Normal Area

You struggled, didn’t you? Me too. Here’s the solution.

Make the substitution u=(x-\tilde\mu)/\sigma. Then x=u\sigma+\tilde\mu and dx=\sigma\,du.

The bounds change with the ruler, and dx=\sigma\,du cancels the factor 1/\sigma.

\begin{aligned} \text{coverage} &= \int_{\mu-1.96\sigma}^{\mu+1.96\sigma} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \sigma} e^{-\frac{(x-\tilde\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} dx \\ &= \int_{-1.96-(\tilde\mu-\mu)/\sigma}^{1.96-(\tilde\mu-\mu)/\sigma} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \sigma} e^{-\frac{u^2}{2}} \sigma du \\ &= \int_{-1.96-(\tilde\mu-\mu)/\sigma}^{1.96-(\tilde\mu-\mu)/\sigma} \textcolor{blue}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{u^2}{2}}} du \end{aligned}

This is the prepared solution to the preceding five-minute exercise. Point out the new endpoints, the exponent becoming u squared, and the two sigma factors canceling. The full calculation is on the slide; there is no need to reconstruct it on the board.

Summary and Generalization

  • We can calculate the coverage of a biased, approximately normal estimator easily.
  • It’s an integral of the standard normal density over the range \pm 1.96 -\text{bias}/\text{se}.
  • The R function call pnorm(x) calculates that integral from -\infty to x. All we need to do is subtract.

Math Version

\small{ \begin{aligned} \text{coverage} &=\int_{-1.96-\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}}}^{+1.96-\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{u^2}{2}} du \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{+1.96-\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{u^2}{2}} du \ - \ \int_{-\infty}^{-1.96-\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{u^2}{2}} du \end{aligned} }

R Version

coverage = function(bias.over.se) {  pnorm(1.96 - bias.over.se) - pnorm(-1.96 - bias.over.se)  }

From Pilot to Follow-up

  • Suppose we’ve got bias, but only a little bit.

  • We’re doing a pilot study with sample size n=100.

  • And we’re sure that our estimator’s absolute bias is less than half its standard error.

  • What coverage can we guarantee?

  • At least 92%. The worst allowed absolute bias is half a standard error; the curve shows about 92% coverage there.
  • Now suppose we’re doing a follow-up study with sample size n=1600.
  • And we’re using the same estimator. Bias is the same.
  • Assume our estimator’s standard error is proportional to \sqrt{1/n}.
    • This is the relation we used for independent sample means last class.
    • That means it’ll be \sqrt{1/1600} \ / \ \sqrt{1/100}=\sqrt{1/16}=1/4 of what it was in the pilot study.
  • What coverage can we guarantee now?
  • A. At least 48%. Here’s why. \text{If } \ \frac{\abs{\text{bias}}}{\text{pilot se}} \le 1/2 \ \text{ and } \ \text{follow-up se} = \frac{\text{pilot se}}{4}, \ \text { then } \ \frac{\abs{\text{bias}}}{\text{follow-up se}} = 4 \times \frac{\abs{\text{bias}}}{\text{pilot se}} \le 2.

That’s a lower guarantee, not a claim that actual coverage must fall to 48%.

Back to Power

  • Remember: two-sided power to rule out a line is one minus the fraction of intervals that cover that line.
  • We already know how to calculate it. Replace bias in SE units by the distance from that line to the estimator’s center.

\text{two-sided power}=1-\text{coverage}(d),\qquad d=\frac{\text{estimator center}-\text{reference line}}{\text{se}}.

Success uses the right tail beyond its cutoff; futility uses the left tail beyond its cutoff. Our 1.96+0.84 construction put 80% in the chosen tail. It’s the same normal-area calculation.

What If They Were Never Exactly Equal?

Illustration: the same gap between two means, with more precision on the right.

  • Return to the original catch-up question: why would the program participants’ mean earnings and their peers’ mean be exactly equal?
  • If they differ, a study may simply lack the power to tell us so. More precision can make a tiny discrepancy easy to detect.
  • But detecting a discrepancy does not tell us whether it came from the program or from the comparison we chose.

The agency asked how close would count as success. Was exact equality ever the interesting question?
